Rig count forecast

Core sample CS-01 · 2,600 to 2,850 ft

Contracts come with short notice and the longest parts take months. How far ahead can the rig count be forecast well enough to act on?

Method
Time-series forecasting (ARIMA, ETS)
Data
US rotary rig count, monthly (EIA, from Baker Hughes)
R packages
forecast, tidyverse, readxl
Status
Logged · Oct 2026
Skip to the forecast replay ↓

01

Project overview

Work in downhole tools can arrive with little notice, while the longest parts take months to come in. I tested how far ahead the US rig count, the most watched measure of drilling activity, can be forecast well enough to act on.

The answer is about one quarter. Out to three months, an ARIMA model misses by about a third less than simply assuming no change. By six months most of that edge is gone, and past eight months the model does no better than assuming no change at all. It also missed both big turns, in late 2014 and early 2020.

So I’d use the forecast as a monthly check on whether to get ready, by refreshing quotes and asking shops about capacity. I would not use it to decide how much to buy. The bill of materials does that.

02

Problem statement

In the build-to-order work I’ve done, parts are bought off the bill of materials once a contract is signed. A forecast can’t tell a buyer how many parts to order, and I wouldn’t use one for that.

The trouble is timing. A contract can land with days or weeks of notice, and the longest parts take months to arrive. Any early sign that work is picking up is worth having, because it tells a buyer when to refresh quotes and ask shops about capacity before the rush.

The rig count is the most watched measure of drilling activity. So the question is: how far ahead can the rig count be forecast well enough to act on, and how would a buyer know when to trust it?

One limit up front. Whether more rigs means more tool orders, and how soon, is an assumption here. I have no public data that ties the two together.

Step 2 · Check for a season

A forecast usually starts by pulling out the season. Before modeling one, I checked whether there is one.

Show the R code 
recent <- rigs %>% filter(date >= as.Date("2000-01-01"))
change <- recent %>%
  mutate(pct = (rigs / lag(rigs) - 1) * 100,
         last_pct = lag(pct),
         month = factor(format(date, "%b"), levels = month.abb)) %>%
  filter(!is.na(last_pct))

season_test <- kruskal.test(pct ~ month, data = change)

bp_both(function(k) {
  ggplot(change, aes(month, pct)) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linewidth = 0.4, colour = k$ink) +
    geom_boxplot(outlier.size = 0.6, outlier.alpha = 0.4, linewidth = 0.5,
                 colour = k$mark, fill = NA, width = 0.6) +
    coord_cartesian(ylim = c(-12, 12)) +
    scale_y_continuous(labels = function(x) paste0(x, "%")) +
    labs(title = "No calendar month stands out, so there is no season to model",
         x = NULL, y = "Change from the month before") +
    theme(panel.grid.major.x = element_blank())
})

A Kruskal-Wallis test finds no difference between calendar months (p = 0.65). US drilling doesn’t stop for winter, so I left the season out of both models.

Step 3 · Find what a model can use

With no season, the question is what pattern is left. The answer is momentum.

The correlation is 0.81. Rigs are contracted and moved over months, so a turn takes time to play out. That lag is the one thing a model of the count alone can learn.

Step 4 · Fit the models and hold back a year

I fit two standard models and one yardstick to the data through August 2025, then forecast the twelve months they had not seen.

  • ARIMA. Predicts next month’s change from this month’s change and last month’s miss. It is built for momentum.
  • ETS (exponential smoothing). Tracks a level and a trend, and weights recent months most.
  • No change. Next month equals this month. Any model has to beat this to be worth running.

Both models work on the log of the count. That keeps their ranges above zero and makes a miss of 50 rigs count for more at 500 rigs than at 2,000.

The model R chose is ARIMA(1,1,1), a plain momentum model. On this one year it tied with assuming no change, and ETS did worse because it carried an old downward trend forward.

One quiet year proves little, though. It says nothing about the years that hurt.

Step 5 · Replay sixteen years of forecasts

So I replayed history. For every month from December 2009 on, I gave each method only the data known that month, had it forecast the next twelve, and scored it against what came after. That is 200 forecasts per method, through booms, two crashes, and long flat spells.

Show the R code 
replay_one <- function(o) {
  known <- ts(y[1:o], start = start(y), frequency = 12)
  f <- list(`No change` = naive(known, h = h, lambda = 0, level = 80),
            ETS         = forecast(ets(known, lambda = 0), h = h, level = 80),
            ARIMA       = forecast(auto.arima(known, lambda = 0, seasonal = FALSE), h = h, level = 80))
  bind_rows(lapply(names(f), function(m) data.frame(
    origin = o, method = m, ahead = 1:h, today = y[o],
    forecast = as.numeric(f[[m]]$mean),
    low  = as.numeric(f[[m]]$lower),          # the 80% range
    high = as.numeric(f[[m]]$upper),
    actual = as.numeric(y[o + 1:h]))))        # NA where the future isn't known yet
}
first_origin <- which(dates == as.Date("2009-12-15"))
replay <- bind_rows(lapply(first_origin:length(y), replay_one))

scored <- replay %>% filter(!is.na(actual)) %>%
  mutate(miss = abs(forecast - actual) / actual * 100,
         inside = actual >= low & actual <= high)

by_ahead <- scored %>% group_by(method, ahead) %>%
  summarise(typical_miss = median(miss), average_miss = mean(miss),
            range_held = mean(inside) * 100, .groups = "drop")

# The model's edge: how much smaller its typical miss is than "no change"
edge <- by_ahead %>% select(method, ahead, typical_miss) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = method, values_from = typical_miss) %>%
  mutate(arima_edge = (1 - ARIMA / `No change`) * 100,
         ets_edge   = (1 - ETS / `No change`) * 100)

04

Results and impact

When to trust it

A forecast is easier to act on as a call than as a number. So I turned each forecast into one: up when the whole 80% range sits above today’s count, down when the whole range sits below it, and no call otherwise. Then I checked the calls against what happened.

Show the R code 
calls <- scored %>% filter(method != "No change") %>%
  mutate(call = case_when(low > today ~ "Up", high < today ~ "Down", TRUE ~ "No call"),
         right = (call == "Up" & actual > today) | (call == "Down" & actual < today))

call_table <- calls %>% filter(ahead %in% c(3, 6, 9, 12)) %>%
  group_by(method, ahead) %>%
  summarise(months = n(), made = sum(call != "No call"),
            right = sum(right[call != "No call"]), .groups = "drop")

call_table %>% arrange(desc(method), ahead) %>%
  transmute(Model = method, `Months ahead` = ahead,
            `Calls made` = paste0(made, " of ", months, " months"),
            `Calls that were right` = paste0(right, " of ", made, " (", round(right / made * 100), "%)")) %>%
  kable(align = "lrrr", caption = "How often each model made a call, and how often the call was right")

Both models stay quiet most months, which is honest: most months, the range includes “about the same.”

Three months out, the calls hold up. ARIMA was right 22 times out of 26, and ETS 29 out of 32.

Past a quarter the two models part ways. At six months ARIMA was right 8 times out of 14, which is close to a coin toss, while ETS was right 20 times out of 24. At most two dozen calls in sixteen years is thin evidence, and the models disagree. So I’d treat a call past a quarter as a hint, not a signal.

I use ARIMA here and in the replay tool. The two models were close on typical miss, ARIMA is the simpler one, and its ranges stay narrow enough to read. A year out, its 80% range runs from 351 to 953 rigs. The ETS range for the same month runs from 200 to 2,044.

From 589 rigs in August 2026, the model expects about 581 three months out, with an 80% range of 501 to 674. That range includes today’s count, so the call is: no call. Six months out the range is 433 to 775, too wide to act on.

What I’d tell a buyer

  1. Run it as a monthly check. It takes a minute once the new count is out, and most months it will say “no call.” That is the right answer most months.
  2. Act on a call only inside a quarter. A three-month call has been right at least five times in six, whichever model made it. Use it to refresh quotes that are about to expire and to ask shops how full they are, on parts that take a quarter or less.
  3. Don’t lean on it for the longest parts. A part that takes six months needs a six-month view. At that distance neither model beats “no change” by much, and the two disagree on which way to call it. That gap has to be closed some other way, with stock, reserved shop time, or supplier agreements. Those are business decisions a forecast can’t make.
  4. Never size an order with it. It says which way activity is heading. The bill of materials says what to buy.

What this doesn’t settle

The model only knows the count’s own past, so it will always be late at a turn. Oil prices tend to move before rigs do, and the next thing I’d test is whether adding them stretches the useful range past a quarter. And as said at the start, the step from rig count to tool orders is an assumption I can’t check with public data.
